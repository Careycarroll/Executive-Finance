# Market Efficiency (EMH Forms)

## Intuition
The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), formulated by Eugene Fama, posits that security prices fully and rapidly reflect all available information. Under market efficiency, active attempts to generate consistent risk-adjusted excess returns ("alpha") without inside information or bearing uncompensated risks are expected to yield zero economic profit after transaction costs.

## Structure
EMH is historically categorized into three nested forms based on the definition of the available information set:

1. **Weak-Form Efficiency:** Current prices reflect all information contained in past trading data (prices, volumes, charts). Implication: Technical analysis cannot generate persistent alpha.
2. **Semi-Strong Form Efficiency:** Current prices rapidly adjust to all publicly available information (earnings releases, 10-K filings, management guidance, macroeconomic data). Implication: Fundamental analysis cannot generate persistent risk-adjusted excess returns.
3. **Strong-Form Efficiency:** Current prices reflect *all* information, both public and private/insider information. Implication: Even corporate insiders cannot beat the market. (Empirically rejected due to SEC enforcement and documented insider trading profits).

## Common Misconceptions
- **Myth 1: "Efficiency means prices are always correct."**  
  *Reality:* Efficiency does not mean prices equal omniscient intrinsic value at every microsecond. It means prices are an unbiased estimate of future fundamentals given current information; forecast errors are randomly distributed with an expected value of zero.
- **Myth 2: "Efficiency implies you cannot get rich in the market."**  
  *Reality:* In an efficient market, you still earn the fair risk-adjusted expected return compensation for bearing market/factor beta risk. You simply cannot expect uncompensated alpha.
- **Myth 3: "A few outperforming managers disprove EMH."**  
  *Reality:* In a population of tens of thousands of fund managers flipping coins over 20 years, a handful will flip heads 20 times in a row purely by random chance (the Berkeley coin-flip experiment).

## Notes for future-me
Gross alpha vs. Net alpha is the crucial real-world distinction (Grossman-Stiglitz paradox). If markets were 100% efficient, nobody would spend money collecting information; but if nobody collects information, markets cannot be efficient. Markets are therefore *near-efficient* up to the cost of gathering and processing information and executing trades.
