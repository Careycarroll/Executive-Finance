Residual Income & EVA Valuation

Primary: Stephen H. Penman Ch. 5, 6 · also: Russell Lundholm Ch. 7
Intuition

Traditional accounting Net Income declares a company "profitable" as long as revenues exceed explicit accounting expenses. However, this ignores the cost of equity capital: shareholders provided equity with an opportunity cost, yet the GAAP income statement charges zero expense for using shareholder equity!

Residual Income (also formulated as Economic Value Added / EVA by Stern Stewart) corrects this fatal blindspot: Residual Income=Accounting Profit−(Capital Employed×Cost of Capital)\text{Residual Income} = \text{Accounting Profit} - (\text{Capital Employed} \times \text{Cost of Capital})Residual Income=Accounting Profit−(Capital Employed×Cost of Capital)

A company only creates true economic wealth when it generates earnings in excess of the dollar cost of all capital invested in the business.

Under the Residual Operating Income (ReOI) model championed by Stephen Penman: Enterprise Value=Current Net Operating Assets (NOA0)+PV of Expected Future Residual Operating Income\text{Enterprise Value} = \text{Current Net Operating Assets (NOA}_0) + \text{PV of Expected Future Residual Operating Income}Enterprise Value=Current Net Operating Assets (NOA0​)+PV of Expected Future Residual Operating Income

This provides a profound mathematical advantage over DCF models: value is anchored immediately on the balance sheet today, shifting the weight of valuation away from subjective terminal year cash flow assumptions.

When to Use / When Not to Use

  • When to use: Valuing capital-intensive businesses, designing executive performance incentive compensation plans (EVA bonuses align managers with shareholders), and cross-checking DCF valuations where terminal value is unnervingly high.
  • When not to use / Caveats: Requires clean-surplus accounting. If a firm engages in off-balance-sheet financing, dirty-surplus accounting (routing large gains/losses directly through Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income instead of the income statement), or excessive restructuring write-offs, book values become distorted and must be adjusted first.
📝 Notes for Future-Me

The beauty of the Residual Income model is how it tames terminal value anxiety. In a DCF, 75%75\%75% of your valuation comes from an imaginary perpetual cash flow 10 years away. In a Residual Income model, the balance sheet gives you today's starting value (NOA0\text{NOA}_0NOA0​), and you only have to estimate the abnormal competitive advantage period (the moat). Under economic theory, competition forces abnormal returns (ReOI\text{ReOI}ReOI) to zero over time as rivals enter. Valuing a business as "Book Value + PV of Moat Duration" is far more intellectually honest than forecasting cash flows to the year 2085.
