Dividend Discount Model (Gordon Growth)

Primary: Zvi Bodie Ch. 18 · also: Russell Lundholm Ch. 3
Intuition

The Dividend Discount Model (DDM) is the foundational valuation model developed by John Burr Williams (1938) and formalized by Myron J. Gordon (1956). It posits that the intrinsic value of a share of common stock equals the present value of all future cash dividends paid to the shareholder in perpetuity.

While Free Cash Flow measures the cash generated by the underlying enterprise, dividends represent the actual cash receipts received by minority equity investors.

Under the Gordon Constant Growth Model, if dividends are assumed to grow at a constant sustainable rate $g$ in perpetuity, the infinite summation collapses into an elegant closed-form formula: $$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r_e - g}$$

This relationship also demonstrates the two fundamental components of an equity investor's expected total return: Dividend Yield plus Capital Gains Growth Rate: $$r_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$

Formula

P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r_e - g} = \frac{D_0 \cdot (1 + g)}{r_e - g} $$g = b \cdot \text{ROE} = (1 - \text{Payout Ratio}) \cdot \text{ROE}

Variable Glossary

  • P_0: Intrinsic value per common share today.
  • D_0: Dividend per share paid in the most recent trailing 12 months.
  • D_1: Expected dividend per share over the next forward 12-month period ($= D_0 \times (1 + g)$).
  • r_e: Required rate of return on common equity (cost of equity from CAPM).
  • g: Sustainable long-term dividend growth rate.
  • b: Earnings retention ratio ($1 - \frac{\text{Dividends}}{\text{Net Income}}$).
  • \text{ROE}: Return on Equity on reinvested retained earnings.

Worked Numerical Example

A mature regulated electric utility currently pays an annual dividend of $$3.20$ per share ($D_0 = $3.20$).

  • Historical Return on Equity ($\text{ROE}$): $11.0%$
  • Dividend Payout Ratio: $65%$ (retention ratio $b = 1 - 0.65 = 0.35$)
  • Cost of Equity ($r_e$ via CAPM): Risk-free rate $4.0% + (\text{Beta } 0.65 \times \text{ERP } 5.5%) = 4.0% + 3.575% = 7.575%$

Step 1: Calculate Sustainable Growth Rate (g) $$g = b \times \text{ROE} = 0.35 \times 11.0% = 3.85% \ (0.0385)$$

Step 2: Calculate Next Year's Expected Dividend (D_1) $$D_1 = $3.20 \times (1 + 0.0385) = $3.3232$$

Step 3: Calculate Intrinsic Share Value (P_0) $$P_0 = \frac{$3.3232}{0.07575 - 0.0385} = \frac{$3.3232}{0.03725} = $89.21$$

If the stock is currently trading on the public exchange at $$76.50$, it trades at a $14.2%$ discount to intrinsic value, offering an attractive margin of safety for income-focused portfolios.

When to Use / When Not to Use

  • When to use: Valuing mature, dividend-paying corporations with predictable payout policies (regulated utilities, real estate investment trusts / REITs, consumer staple conglomerates, and major commercial banking institutions).
  • When not to use / Caveats: Useless for zero-dividend growth stocks, early-stage tech firms, and companies that return capital predominantly via share buybacks rather than cash dividends. For non-dividend payers, always use Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF) or Residual Operating Income.
📝 Notes for Future-Me

The Gordon Growth formula contains an extreme mathematical singularity: as $g$ approaches $r_e$, the denominator $(r_e - g)$ approaches zero, sending the calculated stock price to infinity. Small adjustments in your growth estimate trigger violent swings in valuation. For companies transitioning from high growth to maturity, never force a single $g$ into Gordon Growth; use a Two-Stage or Three-Stage DDM (H-Model) where rapid near-term growth linearly decays over 5 to 10 years to reach the steady-state macroeconomic growth rate.
