Capital Structure & Modigliani-Miller Theorems

Primary: Gabriel Hawawini Ch. 11 · also: Myron Scholes Ch. 6
Overview & Intuition

Capital structure is the mix of long-term debt and equity financing used by an enterprise to fund its overall operations and growth. The central question in corporate finance is: Does the choice of capital structure affect the total market value of the firm, and is there an optimal debt-to-equity ratio that minimizes WACC?

The theoretical foundation was established by Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller (MM, Nobel laureates 1958, 1963):

  1. MM Proposition I (Without Taxes - Irrelevance): In a frictionless world with perfect capital markets, zero taxes, zero bankruptcy costs, and symmetric information, the total market value of a firm is determined solely by the earning power and risk of its underlying assets, completely independent of how those assets are financed (VL=VUV_L = V_UVL​=VU​). Financial leverage merely reshuffles the slices of the pie between debt and equity without changing the size of the pie.
  2. MM Proposition II (Without Taxes - Cost of Equity): As debt increases, the required return on equity (rer_ere​) rises linearly to exactly offset the apparent benefit of cheaper debt: re=r0+DE(r0−rd)r_e = r_0 + \frac{D}{E}(r_0 - r_d)re​=r0​+ED​(r0​−rd​). Consequently, WACC remains perfectly flat across all leverage ratios.
  3. MM Proposition I (With Taxes - Tax Shield): Because interest payments are tax-deductible while equity dividends are not, debt creates a corporate tax shield. The levered firm value equals the unlevered firm value plus the present value of the debt tax shield (VL=VU+t⋅DV_L = V_U + t \cdot DVL​=VU​+t⋅D). In this world without bankruptcy costs, the optimal capital structure would be 100% debt!
  4. Trade-Off Theory (Modern Real World): In reality, increasing debt elevates the probability of financial distress. The Static Trade-Off Theory posits that optimal capital structure balances the marginal tax shield benefit of debt against the marginal expected costs of bankruptcy, debt-holder agency friction, and financial distress.

Key Theories Compared

  • Trade-Off Theory: Firms identify a target debt-equity ratio balancing tax shields against distress costs. High-profit, asset-heavy firms (utilities, real estate) maintain higher debt capacity than volatile R&D firms.
  • Pecking Order Theory (Myers & Majluf): Due to asymmetric information, management follows a strict financing hierarchy: (1) Internal cash flow / retained earnings first, (2) Straight debt second, and (3) External equity issuance only as a desperate last resort to avoid market undervaluation signaling.
⚠️ Common Misconceptions
  • Misconception 1: "Debt is cheaper than equity, so increasing debt always reduces WACC." Reality: Adding debt increases financial leverage risk, driving up the required return on equity (rer_ere​) via the Hamada effect. At high leverage, credit spreads explode. WACC only drops until the optimal debt capacity is reached; beyond that point, WACC rises.
  • Misconception 2: "Bankruptcy costs only matter if the firm actually files for Chapter 11." Reality: Indirect financial distress costs hit long before formal bankruptcy. High leverage causes talented executives and engineers to jump ship, customers to hesitate signing multi-year contracts for fear of orphan software, and suppliers to shorten payment terms from Net-60 to Cash-on-Delivery, strangling working capital.
  • Misconception 3: "A zero-debt company is the most conservatively and optimally run enterprise." Reality: An all-equity firm with stable, predictable operating cash flows leaves substantial tax shields on the table and invites managerial slack (Jensen's Free Cash Flow hypothesis). Moderate leverage imposes capital discipline.
📝 Notes for Future-Me

When sizing debt capacity for your enterprise or an M&A transaction, do not look at peak-cycle EBITDA multiples (e.g., "banks will lend us 5.5×5.5\times5.5× EBITDA today!"). Size debt against a trough-cycle stress test: if revenue contracts by 25% and gross margins compress by 400 basis points, can operating cash flow still cover interest payments by at least 1.5×1.5\times1.5× (Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio)?

Debt is a magnifying glass: it magnifies equity returns on the upside, but it robs management of strategic agility on the downside. The ultimate luxury in a downturn is having dry powder while debt-encumbered competitors are paralyzed by bank covenants.
