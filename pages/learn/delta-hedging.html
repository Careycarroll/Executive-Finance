Dynamic Delta Hedging & Rebalancing Costs

Primary: John C. Hull Ch. 19 · also: Euan Sinclair Ch. 3
Intuition

The entire theoretical architecture of Black-Scholes-Merton option pricing rests on continuous dynamic delta hedging: an option position can be completely replicated or hedged by continuously buying or selling shares of the underlying asset so that the combined portfolio remains delta-neutral (Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0) at every instant.

Under continuous rebalancing in a frictionless world with zero transaction costs, the net P&L of a delta-hedged short option position equals zero: the daily Theta collected from time decay exactly offsets the daily Gamma hedging rebalancing costs.

In the real world, however, continuous rebalancing is impossible:

  1. Discrete Rebalancing: Hedging occurs at discrete intervals (e.g., daily or when delta breaches a threshold bandwidth ±0.05\pm 0.05±0.05). Between rebalancing intervals, the portfolio is exposed to gamma risk.
  2. Transaction Costs & Bid-Ask Drag: Every rebalance incurs brokerage commissions, exchange fees, and market impact costs.
  3. Volatility Mispricing: If an option is sold at an implied volatility σIV\sigma_{\text{IV}}σIV​ and the trader delta-hedges it to expiration, the trader's ultimate profit or loss is determined strictly by the spread between implied volatility and the actual realized volatility (σrealized\sigma_{\text{realized}}σrealized​) of the stock: Expected P&L≈12⋅S2⋅Γ⋅(σIV2−σrealized2)⋅Δt\text{Expected P\&L} \approx \frac{1}{2} \cdot S^2 \cdot \Gamma \cdot (\sigma_{\text{IV}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{realized}}^2) \cdot \Delta tExpected P&L≈21​⋅S2⋅Γ⋅(σIV2​−σrealized2​)⋅Δt

If you sell an option at 30%30\%30% IV and dynamically delta-hedge it while the stock realizes only 20%20\%20% volatility, you lock in pure mathematical profit regardless of which direction the stock moves!

When to Use / When Not to Use

  • When to use: Option market making, institutional execution of large convertible bond hedges, structuring OTC structured note payoffs, and pricing synthetic exotic options.
  • When not to use / Caveats (Jump Risk & Gaps): Delta hedging assumes continuous, smooth price paths (Brownian motion). If a stock announces an overnight surprise acquisition or accounting fraud and gaps down by 40%40\%40% at the opening bell, delta hedging fails completely: your hedge was calibrated for yesterday's price, resulting in massive gap slippage losses.
📝 Notes for Future-Me

Dynamic hedging is a friction trap: rebalancing too frequently (e.g., every 5 minutes) destroys your edge in broker commissions and bid-ask slippage. Rebalancing too infrequently (e.g., once every two weeks) leaves you nakedly exposed to huge unhedged gamma moves.

Use Bandwidth-Based Rebalancing (Whalley-Wilmott bands) instead of fixed-time rebalancing: only execute a rebalancing trade when your net portfolio delta drifts outside a pre-calculated tolerance band: Rebalance only when: ∣Δcurrent−Δtarget∣>(3kΓS2γ)1/3\text{Rebalance only when: } |\Delta_{\text{current}} - \Delta_{\text{target}}| > \left(\frac{3 k \Gamma S}{2 \gamma}\right)^{1/3}Rebalance only when: ∣Δcurrent​−Δtarget​∣>(2γ3kΓS​)1/3 This cuts rebalancing transaction costs by up to 60%60\%60% without materially degrading hedge quality.
