Terminal Value (Exit Multiple vs Perpetuity Growth)

Primary: Russell Lundholm Ch. 6 · also: Stephen H. Penman Ch. 5
Intuition

Because an ongoing business is a going concern expected to generate cash flows in perpetuity, financial models cannot forecast individual line-item income statements out to infinity. Instead, valuation models split time into two phases:

  1. Explicit Forecast Period (t=1…Tt = 1 \dots Tt=1…T): Typically 5 to 10 years, where detailed revenue growth, operational margins, working capital, and capital expenditures are modeled explicitly.
  2. Terminal Period (t>Tt > Tt>T): The infinite horizon beyond year TTT, captured in a single figure: the Terminal Value (TV).

Valuation professionals calculate Terminal Value using two primary methods:

  • Perpetuity Growth Method (Gordon Growth): Assumes the business reaches a mature, steady state where Free Cash Flow grows at a constant, sustainable long-term rate ggg in perpetuity.
  • Exit Multiple Method: Assumes the business is sold or valued at year TTT at a benchmark multiple of final-year EBITDA, NOPAT, or Revenues (e.g., 10.0×10.0\times10.0× EV/EBITDA).

Best practice requires cross-checking both methods: an assumed exit multiple implicitly embeds a perpetual growth rate, and an assumed perpetual growth rate implies an exit multiple. Reconciling the two eliminates absurd hidden assumptions.

When to Use / When Not to Use

  • When to use: Every DCF model, impairment testing of long-lived assets, private equity buyout returns modeling, and fair-value fairness opinions.
  • When not to use / Caveats: Do not assume a perpetual growth rate g>WACCg > \text{WACC}g>WACC! If g≥WACCg \ge \text{WACC}g≥WACC, the Gordon Growth denominator (WACC−g)≤0(\text{WACC} - g) \le 0(WACC−g)≤0, resulting in an infinite or negative mathematical impossibility. 📝 Notes for Future-Me

Beware the "Terminal Year Reinvestment Distortion": In year TTT, models frequently show high growth (e.g., 10% revenue growth) but show CapEx equal to Depreciation and zero working capital changes! In reality, if a company grows at ggg in perpetuity, it MUST reinvest capital to support that growth: Reinvestment Rate=gRONIC\text{Reinvestment Rate} = \frac{g}{\text{RONIC}}Reinvestment Rate=RONICg​ If you assume a 3% perpetual growth rate and a 10% Return on New Invested Capital (RONIC), the firm MUST reinvest 30%30\%30% of its NOPAT every year (3%/10%=30%3\% / 10\% = 30\%3%/10%=30%). If your model assumes g=3%g = 3\%g=3% but gives the company 100% free cash flow conversion, you are creating phantom value out of thin air.
