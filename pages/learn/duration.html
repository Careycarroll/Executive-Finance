Macaulay and Modified Duration

Primary: Zvi Bodie Ch. 16 · also: Gabriel Hawawini Ch. 12
Intuition

A bond's maturity date states when the final principal is repaid, but it tells an executive very little about how sensitive the bond's market value is to interest rate swings. A 30-year bond paying a massive 10% coupon returns most of its cash flows in early years, making it far less volatile than a 30-year zero-coupon bond that pays nothing until year 30.

Frederick Macaulay (1938) solved this by introducing Duration:

  1. Macaulay Duration (DMacD_{\text{Mac}}DMac​): The weighted-average time (in years) until a bond's cash flows are received, where the weights are the present values of each cash flow divided by the total bond price.
  2. Modified Duration (D∗D^*D∗): The first derivative of bond price with respect to yield. It measures the percentage change in bond price for a 100 basis point (1%) shift in yield-to-maturity: ΔPP≈−D∗⋅Δy\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \cdot \Delta yPΔP​≈−D∗⋅Δy

If a bond portfolio has a Modified Duration of 7.5 years7.5\text{ years}7.5 years, a 100 bps100\text{ bps}100 bps (+1.0%+1.0\%+1.0%) rise in market interest rates causes an immediate ≈7.5%\approx 7.5\%≈7.5% drop in the portfolio's market value.

📝 Notes for Future-Me

Duration is a linear first-order approximation. For tiny rate movements (±10−25 bps\pm 10-25\text{ bps}±10−25 bps), duration predicts price changes with near-flawless accuracy. But because the price-yield curve is convex (curved upward), for large interest rate shocks (≥150−200 bps\ge 150-200\text{ bps}≥150−200 bps), duration systematically underestimates price gains when rates fall and overestimates price drops when rates rise!

To price large interest rate shocks accurately, always combine Modified Duration with Convexity: ΔPP≈−D∗⋅Δy+12⋅Convexity⋅(Δy)2\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \cdot \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{Convexity} \cdot (\Delta y)^2PΔP​≈−D∗⋅Δy+21​⋅Convexity⋅(Δy)2 Without the convexity adjustment, corporate hedging programs will over-hedge rate hikes and leave portfolios under-protected.
